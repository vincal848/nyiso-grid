# Structure beats architecture in NYISO price forecasting

The best-evidenced way to forecast LMPs is not a single better architecture. It is an **ensemble of domain-specific models (LEAR across several calibration windows plus a tuned feedforward DNN or NBEATSx), fed with fundamentals and wrapped in a probabilistic post-processing layer (QRA, conformal prediction, isotonic distributional regression)**. On the only US dataset in the canonical benchmark (PJM COMED DA), that recipe beats LEAR by roughly 3-8% rMAE, and distributional or post-processed variants beat LEAR-QRA by 7-17% CRPS in Europe. Industry desks work differently. They combine production-cost or SCUC replication (Dayzer, PROMOD, PLEXOS), vintaged constraint, outage and shadow-price data, and historical congestion analysis. No vendor publishes independent accuracy figures. For the two places where your desk is losing, congestion and RT spikes, the literature and the NYISO market monitor point to the same fix: stop fitting squared-loss models to zero-inflated, heavy-tailed targets. Instead, build **factorized two-part models**. For congestion, that means P(constraint binds) × E[shadow price | binds], mapped to nodes through shift factors. For spikes, it means P(spike) × a magnitude model with an EVT tail, driven by reserve-shortage, outage, thunderstorm-alert and load-forecast-error features. Those models belong in M3 and in a new spike track, ahead of the deep and graph models in M6. GNNs and foundation models have the weakest real-ISO evidence of any family reviewed. A caveat runs through everything below. Almost all rigorous benchmark evidence is European DA. US RT, nodal and congestion-component forecasting has almost no peer-reviewed horse races, so your 36-fold backtest is closer to the frontier than most published work.

## Benchmarks rank ensembles of domain models first, and the PJM gap is 3-8%

The reference point is Lago, Marcjasz, De Schutter and Weron's 2021 open benchmark. It covers five markets over two-year test periods, and its one US dataset is PJM COMED zonal DA prices with system and zonal load forecasts as inputs ([arXiv 2008.08004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08004)). The ranking it reports has been replicated repeatedly: **DNN ensemble > best single DNN > LEAR ensemble > best single LEAR**. In PJM the rMAE values are 0.439, 0.467, 0.476 and 0.489 respectively. So averaging LEAR over windows of 56 days, 84 days, 3 years and 4 years gains about 3%, and the DNN ensemble adds about 8% on top. Multivariate Giacomini-White tests find the DNN ensemble significantly better than every other model in all five markets ([arXiv 2008.08004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08004)). NBEATSx, the interpretable neural basis-expansion model with exogenous inputs, adds a further **~2% rMAE over the DNN ensemble on average, but the gain in PJM (0.24%) is not significant** ([arXiv 2104.05522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05522)). The honest reading for a US desk is that NBEATSx ≈ DNN, and that the value comes from ensembling rather than from the specific architecture.

Your results fit this picture. LEAR winning DA totals and LightGBM edging it on RT matches Ziel and Weron's finding that per-hour ("multivariate") and pooled ("univariate") LASSO frameworks each win on some datasets, and that combining them beats both ([doi:10.1016/j.eneco.2017.12.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eneco.2017.12.016)). Your pooled LightGBM is the pooled model and LEAR is the per-hour model, so the two are natural ensemble partners. There is no peer-reviewed, controlled comparison of LightGBM against LEAR or DNN on the benchmark datasets. The evidence that GBMs win comes from non-peer-reviewed repositories, such as a Turkish DA project ([GitHub](https://github.com/beratkrts/electricity-price-forecasting-turkish-day-ahead-market)). Your RT result is therefore itself a useful data point, not a confirmation of published findings.

Two calibration details matter. First, Lipiecki and Weron report that choosing λ by 7-fold cross-validation is "more accurate" than the LARS-based selection in the original epftoolbox LEAR ([arXiv 2609.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00089)). Second, the Weron group's replicated window-averaging result is that mixing a few very short windows (about 28-56 days) with long windows (about 2-3 years) beats any single window ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/11/9/2364); [doi:10.1109/TSTE.2018.2869557](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSTE.2018.2869557)). Refitting monthly on three windows is lighter than the benchmark practice of daily re-estimation over four or more windows ([arXiv 2008.08004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08004)). Lago et al. also warn against RMSE and MAPE, recommend rMAE against a naive-1 benchmark with Diebold-Mariano or Giacomini-White tests on daily loss vectors, and suggest ensembles of about 4-10 diverse members. Your -21% MAE versus yesterday's DA is roughly an rMAE of 0.79, but epftoolbox's naive-1 uses the previous week for Monday, Saturday and Sunday. Recompute it exactly before comparing with the benchmark's PJM LEAR figure of about 0.48.

Time-series foundation models are where hype and evidence diverge most. Zero-shot univariate models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, TimeGPT, Time-MoE) **did not statistically beat a simple biseasonal MSTL baseline** on 2024 European DA prices ([arXiv 2506.08113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08113)). On CAISO 2025 data chosen to postdate pretraining, covariate-aware foundation models beat generic deep learning, but the **simple average of Chronos-2 (with covariates) and CING-LEAR was the best model overall**. Generic deep models such as TFT, DeepAR and TiDE did not beat domain EPF methods ([arXiv 2607.02623](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.02623)). The strongest positive result so far is Lipiecki and Weron's five-year, three-market test. There, **only the TabPFN family consistently beat LEAR+CP and DDNN-JSU**, with TabPFN-3 improving CRPS by 12-15% over DDNN-JSU. Even so, DDNN-JSU was more profitable under risk-averse bidding ([arXiv 2609.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00089)). The only NYISO-specific foundation-model result, GridFM on RT 5-minute LMP, compared itself only against other foundation models and reported MAPE ([via arXiv 2609.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00089)). That is not evidence against LEAR. Also note that the 2026 deep-learning review explicitly excludes US markets and contains no head-to-head quantitative benchmark ([arXiv 2602.10071](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10071v2)).

## Post-processing ensembles, not better residual quantiles, fix under-coverage

Your 90% intervals cover about 88% and your 98% intervals about 95-96%. That is the textbook symptom of static empirical-residual quantiles, which amount to naive split conformal, applied to non-exchangeable, regime-dependent errors. The literature offers two families that work. The first **post-processes point forecasts**: Quantile Regression Averaging (QRA) and its LASSO, smoothed and isotonic variants, conformal prediction, and isotonic distributional regression (IDR). The second is **distributional neural networks**, most notably a DNN that outputs Johnson's SU parameters for each hour. In German DA tests, DDNN-JSU beat DNN-QRA by about 7% CRPS and LEAR-QRA by about 17%, and raised per-transaction trading profit by 8%. Single-network runs varied by up to 10% in CRPS, so run-ensembling is mandatory ([arXiv 2207.02832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02832)). The same table shows something easy to miss: QRA on DNN point forecasts scored 1.399 CRPS against 1.575 on LEAR. **The quality of the point models feeding the post-processor matters more than the post-processor.**

The strongest single result for M4 comes from Lipiecki, Uniejewski and Weron (2024). **An ensemble of QRA + conformal prediction + IDR, applied to point forecasts, beat DDNN** over two 4.5-year test periods in Germany and Spain, including COVID and the 2022 crisis. IDR contributed most by Shapley value ([arXiv 2404.02270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02270)). Isotonic QRA claims better reliability than coverage-based conformal on German data, but it is a single study with no numbers in the abstract ([arXiv 2507.15079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.15079)). On the Irish DA and balancing markets, which are the closest European analogue to RT, O'Connor et al. found that QR and QRA intervals were narrow but **fell short in coverage**. Conformal methods (split CP, EnbPI, SPCI) hit coverage, and a QR+CP ensemble got both, along with the best battery-trading returns ([arXiv 2502.04935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.04935)). Adaptive conformal inference updates the miscoverage rate online and has been demonstrated on French spot prices ([arXiv 2202.07282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.07282)).

For the extreme quantiles your RT signal needs (0.98 and above), quantile regression runs out of data, and conditioning on covariates makes this worse ([arXiv 2103.10335](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.10335)). The evidence-backed alternatives are these. Fitting a GPD tail (EVT) to filtered residuals gives more accurate extreme quantiles than normal- or t-innovation GARCH ([IJF 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207005001226)). Training on the unfiltered series is better for outer quantiles in the volatile South Australian market, where a QRA ensemble across model classes and window lengths beat every constituent and AEMO's own pre-dispatch forecast ([arXiv 2311.07289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.07289)). Two evaluation cautions apply. Some papers report the average pinball loss over 99 percentiles as "CRPS", which is half the true value. And statistical and economic rankings disagree often enough that a trading-value metric belongs in every model comparison ([arXiv 2609.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00089)).

## Industry forecasts price by replicating the ISO, but publishes no accuracy

Commercial LMP forecasting sits on production-cost and SCUC/SCED replication. Dayzer states that it "mimic[s] the dispatch procedures used by the corresponding ISOs". It forecasts DA zonal and nodal LMPs, shadow prices and congestion costs from fuel prices, demand forecasts, unit and line outages and emissions costs, and it covers NYISO ([CES Dayzer](https://www.ces-us.com/product-dayzer.asp)). PROMOD forecasts nodal, hub and zonal LMP and is marketed as a long-standing "gold standard", but the source is a blog comparison, not an accuracy study ([blog](https://futureofcomputing.beehiiv.com/p/tool-use-production-cost-modeling-promod-versus-plexos)). **No independent accuracy study of PROMOD, PLEXOS, Dayzer, Aurora, MAPS or PowerSIMM against realized US LMPs was found.** The clearest public validation methodology is European. The Irish SEM Committee's PLEXOS backcasts warn that a single year of data cannot validate a model, and that over-fitting to one historical year makes a model "not appropriate to model future expected years" ([SEM-20-004](https://www.semcommittee.com/files/semcommittee/media-files/SEM-20-004%20SEM%20PLEXOS%20Validation%20(2019-2025)%20and%20Backcast%20Report.pdf)). Vendor Yes Energy makes the same point about data: backtests need vintaged, point-in-time data ([Yes Energy](https://www.yesenergy.com/blog/the-importance-of-vintaging-locational-marginal-pricing-data)). For a public-data desk, simulation is most useful for structural what-if analysis (outages, new transmission) at monthly-to-annual horizons, not for hourly point accuracy.

The lighter-weight fundamental alternative is the **bid-stack or heat-rate model**. Price is where an aggregate inverse supply curve, built from fuel price × heat rate by marginal fuel, meets inelastic demand. Multi-fuel versions yield closed-form derivative prices, which makes them natural for the forward-curve work in M5 ([arXiv 1205.2299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1205.2299)). NYISO supplies unusually good calibration data. Its masked generator bids are published with a 3-month lag and carry trackable masked IDs ([NYISO bid data description](http://mis.nyiso.com/public/postings/NYISO%203%20Month%20Bid%20Data%20Release%20Description_V1.pdf)), and EIA-923 and EPA CEMS provide heat rates, although the two sources disagree ([EIA-923](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/data/eia923/); [Andover](https://www.andovertechnology.com/comparison-of-cems-and-fuel-data-for-heat-rate/)). The 3-month lag rules masked offers out as a next-day input but suits calibrating stack shape and markup. No public error metrics for a NYISO supply-stack LMP model were found.

For congestion, vendors sell **data and workflow rather than forecasts**: historical DA/RT shadow prices, shift factors, FTR auction constraint data, outage feeds and new-build trackers ([Yes Energy FTR](https://www.yesenergy.com/ftr-trading-solutions); [Infrastructure Insights](https://www.yesenergy.com/products/infrastructure-insights)). All of this is vendor marketing with no accuracy claims. FTR trader job ads describe the stack indirectly: power-flow and simulation tools (PowerWorld, Dayzer, UPLAN), plus historical congestion, outage and fundamentals research ([SimplyHired](https://www.simplyhired.com/k-ftr-trader-jobs.html)). No trading firm publicly documents its models.

What the market monitor's data shows is that **NYISO congestion is a regime problem more than a fitting problem**. DA congestion revenue was $306M in 2024, when Central-East's share collapsed from 53% to 24% after the AC Transmission projects came online and NYC congestion tripled to $52M on outages ([2024 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/NYISO-2024-SOM-Full-Report_5-14-2025-final.pdf)). It then jumped **108% to $638M in 2025**, driven by gas prices and inter-regional gas spreads, with nearly 70% concentrated in January, February and December ([2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). TCC holders lost 47% in one capability year and gained 135% in the next. The monitor attributes both swings to gas-spread and outage regimes "not well anticipated at the time of the auctions" ([2024 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/NYISO-2024-SOM-Full-Report_5-14-2025-final.pdf); [2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). A single-gas-hub heat-rate model will miss this. Regional gas spreads (for example, Transco Z6 NY against western hubs), outage schedules for a short list of facilities, and Quebec imports need to be explicit features.

### Where DART and TCC edge comes from

NYISO virtuals trade only at the 11 load zones, plus virtual-like imports and exports at proxy buses ([2024 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/NYISO-2024-SOM-Full-Report_5-14-2025-final.pdf)). Nodal forecasts therefore matter for TCCs and for understanding how zonal prices form, not for placing virtuals directly. Virtual profits averaged **$0.06/MWh in calm 2024 and $1.02/MWh ($30.3M net) in volatile 2025**. Southeast NY carried structural RT premiums in 2025, with NYC at +4.7% and Long Island at +5.6% ([2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). The academic record says edge comes as much from market design as from forecast skill. Parsons et al. show virtuals can profit from ISO modeling simplifications ([MIT CEEPR](https://www.mit.edu/~jparsons/publications/20150300_Financial_Arbitrage_and_Efficient_Dispatch.pdf)). Birge et al. find DART arbitrage in MISO limited by capital and transaction costs ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0140988318303529)). In NYISO, thunderstorm-alert constraints modeled in RT but not DA are a documented, predictable source of divergence ([2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). For TCCs, Leslie's study of 235 NYISO auctions (2006-2015) finds zonal TCCs priced at actuarially fair levels. Financial-trader profits came from nodal products, **88% of them from being first to buy previously illiquid paths**, and prices rose about 10% once a profitable purchase was revealed ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2019/preliminary/paper/keh5iRB4)). Early NYISO TCCs cleared at under half of ARCH-ARMA-predicted value ([Adamson & Englander](https://www.longwoodenergy.com/sites/default/files/docs/efficiency_of_new_york_transmission_congestion_contract_auctions.pdf)). That evidence is 2000-2015, and no recent NYISO auction-efficiency study was found.

## Congestion needs a factorized structural model, because active sets almost never repeat

Theory gives the target a clean structure. The congestion part of the nodal price matrix factors into network sensitivity × a sparse shadow-price matrix, because only a few lines bind at once ([arXiv 1410.6095](https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.6095)). That factorization was validated only on the IEEE 30-bus system. The one large real-ISO recovery, Birge, Hortaçsu and Pavlin's inverse optimization on MISO 2010-2012, recovered shift-factor-like constraint utilizations and reconstructed prices with **median correlation 0.92, but with about 11% absolute error even with bid data** ([Operations Research](https://epic.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2016/12/opre.2017.1606.pdf)). Their data also show how sparse binding is. Of 1,295 lines that hit capacity in 2012, **89% bound in 1% of hours or fewer**, and none bound more than 30% of the time. The research brief reports that your preliminary OLS estimate of MCC from shadow prices has a median out-of-sample R² of about 0.75 and a low-rank (about 8-component) structure. That sits near the frontier of what has been shown on real data. The hard part is not the shift factors. It is forecasting the shadow prices.

This is why **per-pattern approaches will not transfer**. Ji, Thomas and Tong's multiparametric method, which the M3 plan cites, is exact but operator-side: it needs the full network model and offers. It was tested only on 3-bus and IEEE-118 systems, and the authors describe the barriers for outside participants as "daunting" ([arXiv 1503.06171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.06171)). Its participant-side cousins either used synthetic grids ([Geng & Xie, arXiv 1603.07276](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.07276)) or a NYISO 2007 test with five constraints and two test days, benchmarked against GARCH and not against persistence ([Zhou, Tesfatsion & Liu](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/WorkingPaper-CongestionForecasting.ZTL2010.pdf)). The ML-for-OPF active-set literature assumes a few sets carry most of the probability mass. That held on most PGLib cases (fewer than 10 sets), but on "adversarial" cases **thousands of sets were discovered** ([arXiv 1802.09639](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.09639v4)). The research brief reports about 4,655 distinct binding sets in 8,760 hours for your data, which puts real NYISO firmly in that high-complexity regime.

What does transfer is the multiparametric insight. Within a fixed active set, shadow prices are piecewise constant or affine in injections, and outages act as a mixture over system configurations ([arXiv 1503.06171](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.06171)). The practical M3 design, which is an inference from these sources rather than a published pipeline, is a **per-constraint hurdle model** for the top 50-100 constraints. The first part is a regularized logistic or GBM model of P(bind) driven by interface flow/limit ratios, scheduled outages mapped to constraints, zonal load and weather, imports, gas spreads, and (for RT) DA binding status. The second is a quantile model of log shadow price given binding. The two are combined through pooled reduced-rank shift factors re-estimated per topology epoch, with rare constraints grouped into a residual term, and joint simulation used for quantiles because constraints co-bind. That design treats persistence as a feature rather than a competitor. It explains why LEAR and LightGBM lose on raw MCC: a squared-loss model on a mostly-zero, heavy-tailed target collapses toward zero or the mean. It also delivers TCC values directly as the sum over constraints of path shift-factor differential × expected cumulative shadow price. **No peer-reviewed real-ISO study reports binding-classification accuracy, a hurdle shadow-price model, or the quantitative value of outage schedules.** This is original work, and the evaluation should use Brier and log loss for binding, pinball loss for congestion, and persistence plus hour-by-month climatology baselines.

The M3 plan's congestion regime Markov chain has weaker support than the hurdle model. Markov regime-switching models fit spike dynamics well in-sample; Janczura and Weron's best specification was a 3-regime model with time-varying transition probabilities ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v32y2010i5p1059-1073.html)). No 2020-2026 benchmark shows regime-switching models winning out of sample, and no Markov-switching model of transmission shadow prices on real ISO data was found. A Markov chain with time-varying transitions is essentially a logit on covariates. The binding classifier subsumes it, so use filtered regime probabilities as features rather than as the forecaster.

GNNs are the weakest-evidenced family here. Nodal-LMP GNN papers use IEEE-118 or small real datasets and weak baselines (ARIMA, plain LSTM). None benchmarks against persistence or LEAR on the congestion component ([arXiv 2107.12794](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12794); [arXiv 2301.09038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09038)). Spatial effects do help across 39 European bidding zones ([arXiv 2606.07014](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07014)), but that is a zonal coupling problem, not nodal congestion. Because your shift factors already capture the spatial structure linearly, a graph convolution would mostly re-learn them.

## RT spikes reward sparse occurrence models and ORDC-aware magnitudes

Losing to persistence in the top-5% RT hours is what the spike literature predicts. Spikes cluster ("it never rains but it pours"), so persistence captures that clustering for free, while models trained on average loss shrink toward the mean ([SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.5547/ISSN0195-6574-EJ-Vol30-No1-2)). The field separates **occurrence** from **magnitude**. Occurrence is modeled with dynamic logit, autoregressive conditional hazard or zero-inflated models, mostly on the Australian NEM ([IJF 2012](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfor:v:28:y:2012:i:2:p:400-411); [J. Energy Markets 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257947871_Models_for_short-term_forecasting_of_spike_occurrences_in_Australian_electricity_markets_A_comparative_study)). Magnitude is modeled with conditional distributions and EVT tails. The most relevant US evidence is Hubert, Lolas and Sircar's out-of-sample study of NYISO, ISO-NE and ERCOT over 2022-2025. **Regularized logistic classifiers on lagged DART, load forecasts and lagged load-forecast errors beat random forests, gradient boosting and neural networks on out-of-sample trading**, because spikes are too rare for flexible models to learn stably ([arXiv 2601.05085](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05085)). Their NYISO classifiers were deliberately selective (about 4-5% recall). The P&L was concentrated on Long Island, and a single heat-wave day (24 June 2025) supplied most of it. The two research notes disagree on the NYISO total ($5.76M net of price impact versus $6.8M), so check the paper before quoting it. NYISO zonal DART spreads are far less correlated with each other than ERCOT's (above 0.97), which argues for zone-specific models.

The NYISO market monitor identifies the physical drivers, and all of them can be partly observed from public data. **Reserve shortages occurred in about 7% of RT intervals in 2025 and raised average LBMPs by 10-15%.** Transmission shortages occurred in about 20% of intervals, ranging from under 2% in the North to over 12% on Long Island. Thunderstorm alerts cut upstate-to-downstate transfer capability by 1-2 GW and are modeled in RT but not in DA, and **50 of 1,377 high-risk hours produced 99% of thunderstorm-alert congestion costs**. The monitor's weather-based thunderstorm model showed "strong predictive accuracy" ([2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). NYISO's shortage prices are also low and stepwise, $750-3,000/MWh even in deep shortage against $4,000-12,000/MWh in ISO-NE and PJM. NYISO spikes are therefore mostly frequent, moderate, operating-reserve-demand-curve (ORDC) step adders plus local congestion, not rare events at the price cap ([2025 SOM](https://www.potomaceconomics.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NYISO-2025-SOM-Report__5-19-2026-final.pdf)). That makes a semi-structural magnitude model workable: base energy price + Σ P(shortage of product k) × ORDC step value + congestion. The Dynamic Reserve redesign targeted for 2028 will be a structural break.

Hawkes and self-exciting spike models show out-of-sample skill only outside the US, for example in Japan's JEPX ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/1996-1073/16/4/1570)). The cheap way to capture their benefit is to add exponentially decayed counts of recent spikes and shortage intervals as classifier features. For DART, model the spread's conditional distribution directly rather than differencing two independent forecasts. Differencing discards the strong DA/RT error correlation. This is inferred from the sources rather than tested, and no study has fitted a copula to DA and RT at the same US node. The European copula evidence (skew-t marginals, significant tail dependence) comes from pairs of coupled DA markets, not from DA-RT pairs ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/eneeco/v68y2017icp283-302.html)).

## Conclusion

The central finding is that your two open problems are target-design problems, not capacity problems. Congestion and RT spikes are zero-inflated, clustered and regime-driven. Every documented win on such targets, from spike logits to structural shadow-price recovery to NYISO's own thunderstorm and shortage analysis, comes from making the physics explicit and keeping the occurrence model sparse. More expressive learners do not help. That argues for reordering the roadmap: pull the hurdle congestion model and a spike track ahead of M6, treat the GNN as optional, and bring DNN, NBEATSx and TabPFN in as ensemble members rather than replacements. The steady DA-accuracy gains the literature does document (window and cross-family averaging, post-processing ensembles) are cheap enough to take in M2 and M4.

The second implication is commercial. The monitor's data show NYISO edge arriving in regime shifts (gas spreads, long outages, heat waves) and in illiquid nodal TCC paths. Edge from shaving average MAE is small: virtuals earned $0.06/MWh in a calm year. A forecast signal built for DART and TCCs should therefore be judged on its calibrated tails and its regime detection, scored by P&L that is robust to single events, more than on headline MAE. Because almost none of the published evidence covers US RT, nodal or congestion-component forecasting, the desk's own rolling backtests are the evidence that will decide these questions.
